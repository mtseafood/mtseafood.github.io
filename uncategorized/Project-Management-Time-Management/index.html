Milestone

Some activities are far more important and representative than others, so we treat them as milestones to see how far we are from our plans. Examples include graduating from college, getting married, having children, starting a business, and retiring.

Rolling Wave Planning

This is probably the term in PMBOK that is closest to Agile. It uses a method of Progressive Elaboration, where we plan in detail and specifically for the near term, while keeping the long-term plans rough and abstract. For instance, at the age of twelve, one might think about how to study in junior high school to best benefit their future and establish specific plans, whereas thinking about the details of marriage or college right now may simply be speculative and a waste of time.

Schedule Compression

Under the premise of not changing the project scope, the goal is to shorten the overall duration of the project. There are two common methods:

  1. Crashing: This means spending money to buy time, such as hiring an excellent tutor to make courses easier to understand. Or, in the case of marriage, offering a bride price and a house to reduce the time spent finding a girlfriend. It’s important to note that while the project timeline may shorten, the goal may not be achieved. For instance, if the original intention was to find someone who loves you for marriage, it might change to seeking someone who loves money instead. However, if the only goal is to get married to anyone, then it doesn’t matter.

  2. Fast Tracking: This involves changing originally sequential activities to be carried out in parallel. A common example is a part-time job while studying, or studying at university while having children. Common way to estimate the time

  1. Expert Judgment: Consult familiar team members or external experts for evaluations.
  2. Analogous Estimation: Estimate time based on similar past projects.
  3. Parametric Estimation: Use historical data parameters for calculations, suitable for simple tasks. For example, cabinet installation time is estimated using dimensions, but higher carpentry complexity increases variability.
  4. Three-Point Estimation: Divide estimates into “most likely,” “pessimistic,” and “optimistic” values, useful for projects with risks and uncertainties.
  5. Bottom-Up Estimation: Start estimating from the lowest Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) level and accumulate upwards.

Scrum

Story Points Represent: “Time Scale” of Tasks

"People actually do not have much ability to estimate the time required for big tasks."

Each Sprint may contain dozens of Stories, and each Story has its own Story Point used to measure the “time scale” of a single task. The assessment of the time scale includes factors such as “complexity,” “number of people involved,” “familiarity,” “difficulty,” and “basic operation time.” Generally, the more complex the task, the more people involved, and the less familiar it is… the more time it will take.

Since it is a measure of “time scale,” it, of course, “excludes” the value brought by completing the task, the money that needs to be spent, and so on.

Simple Process for Story Point Estimation

Estimating story points is similar to a simple show of hands. Here’s our company’s process:

  1. Complete Story Overview:
    Begin the planning meeting by discussing all aspects of the stories (goals, methods, criteria, etc.).

  2. Establish Criteria for Evaluation:
    Set foundational standards using a couple of stories to create a consensus. For example, if you think a story is a 5-point and I think it’s a 13-point, we aim to adjust them to a common team score, like a 5.

  3. Individual Scoring:
    Members score the stories independently on a form using Fibonacci numbers (1-21). This is done silently, without discussion or questions. After everyone has submitted their scores, discussion can begin.

    (If there are issues, use “?” or “21” to highlight concerns, as asking questions afterward would be redundant.)

  4. Discuss Significant Discrepancies:
    Focus on stories with large differences in scores where consensus is not reached.
